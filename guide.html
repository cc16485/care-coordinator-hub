Is this person eligible for GUIDE?

Nine questions. Ask them on the phone while you have the family, in this order, because the first two knock out most people and there is no point going further.

This screening is ours, not Medicare's. It exists so you do not drive across town for an assessment that was never going to qualify. Nobody is enrolled by anything on this page. Enrollment is a patient record and a comprehensive assessment in Crosby Health, submitted by noon on a Friday. Email is only for when something is blocking you.

What changed on June 1, 2026

The July 2026 Amended Participation Agreement limits GUIDE to people living in a private residence or independent living. Everyone in a residential care community is now ineligible: assisted living, memory care, group homes, adult family homes, board and care.

EmpowerMe also stopped enrolling RCC patients altogether, because CMS's new RCC partner requirements ask communities for ownership structures, leadership Social Security numbers, and three years of compliance history. Almost no senior living provider will agree to that.

Three things this means for you: stop assessments for anyone in memory care or an RCC. Verify the residence type for anyone in independent living, and be ready to attest in Crosby that their community is not an RCC. And never request unenrollment for a current patient in memory care or an RCC. EmpowerMe handles all of those. The only exceptions are moving out, hospice, or death.

1. Has a doctor diagnosed dementia or memory impairment?
Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, or a general memory-impairment diagnosis all count. A family saying "she is forgetful" does not. It has to be in a chart somewhere.
2. What kind of Medicare do they have?
This is the one that disqualifies the most people. Traditional Medicare only. If the card says Aetna, Humana, UnitedHealthcare, Anthem, Wellcare, or any plan name at all, that is Medicare Advantage and they cannot be aligned.
3. Where do they live?
This rule changed on June 1, 2026. GUIDE is now limited to a private residence or independent living. Residential care communities are out, which means assisted living, memory care, group homes, and board and care homes. If you learned this job before June, unlearn that part.
4. Are they on the Medicare hospice benefit?
Hospice rules them out. Palliative care does not, and families mix the two up constantly, so ask which one it is by name.
5. Are they enrolled in PACE?
Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly. Rare around here, but it rules them out, and it is one of the reasons people get unaligned later.
6. Is there an unpaid caregiver?
A spouse, an adult child, a neighbor. Somebody who is not paid to be there. The respite money exists to give that person a break, so if there is nobody, respite has nothing to relieve and it is a common rejection reason.
7. Are they already aligned with another GUIDE provider?
A person can only be aligned with one. If another agency or clinic already enrolled them, they are not available to us.
8. Do they also have Medicaid or VA benefits?
Neither one disqualifies anybody. Note it so billing is not surprised later.
9. Are they inside our service area?
We can only take the respite hours if we can staff the visits. If they are outside it, they may still be eligible, and Continuum can align them with a different partner.

The summary

What to say

Three different conversations. The words matter less than the order: what it is, what it pays for, what it costs them. Say the cost part early, because everybody is waiting for the catch.

The page to send them

Families can check eligibility themselves at mo-care.com/guide, which is the short link on the flyers and lands on the full page. Whatever they answer comes to us as a lead with the answers attached, so you already know where they stand before you call.

Open it

The one sentence, if you only get one

Medicare started a new program for people with dementia, and it pays for someone to come sit with your mother so you can leave the house. It does not cost you anything, and it does not replace her doctor.

Do not lead with the name. "GUIDE" means nothing to a family and sounds like a brochure. Lead with what it does.

To a client we already have

Easiest money on this list. They already trust us and we already know the diagnosis.

You: While I have you, there is something new I want to make sure you know about. Medicare added a dementia program this year called GUIDE. Because your mom has a dementia diagnosis and traditional Medicare, she almost certainly qualifies.

Them: What does it do?

You: Three things. A dementia nurse who checks in with you every month, a 24-hour line you can call at two in the morning when something happens, and about $2,600 a year of respite care that Medicare pays for. That last part means roughly eighteen four-hour visits from us that do not come out of your pocket.

Them: What does it cost?

You: Nothing. It is a Medicare benefit. It does not change her doctor, it does not change her plan, and it does not touch the hours you are already paying for. It sits on top of them.

You: There are about nine questions I need to run through to confirm, and it takes five minutes. Do you have those five minutes now or should I call you back this evening?

To somebody calling us for the first time

Listen for dementia in the first two minutes. "She keeps repeating herself," "he got lost driving," "we had to take the stove knobs off." Then ask the Medicare question before you quote a rate.

You: Before we talk about hours and cost, let me ask you two things that could save you money. Has a doctor told you it is dementia or Alzheimer's? … And is she on traditional Medicare, or does she have a plan through Humana or Aetna or one of those?

You: Good. Then there is a Medicare program she likely qualifies for that pays for part of her care. Most people have never heard of it because it is new. It will not cover everything you need, so we will still put together a regular schedule, but it will cover about $2,600 a year of it and it comes with a dementia nurse and a 24-hour line.

You: Here is how it usually goes. We get care started now, because the paperwork takes a few weeks and you need help this week. Once Medicare aligns her, part of it gets billed to them instead of to you.

That last paragraph is the whole play. Start private pay, convert part of it to GUIDE later. Never make a family wait weeks for help they need now.

To a referral source

Discharge planners, memory clinics, home health, elder law, social workers. Most of them do not know GUIDE exists, and that is your whole opening.

You: Do you know about the GUIDE program? … Most people do not. It is a Medicare model for dementia patients on traditional Medicare. It gives the family a dementia care navigator, a 24-hour clinical line, and about $2,600 a year of paid respite.

You: The reason I am telling you is that we are an approved respite provider for it, so when you have a dementia discharge going home with a worn-out spouse, you have somewhere to send them that costs the family nothing. I am not asking you to sell anything. I am asking you to remember it exists.

You: Can I leave you a few one-pagers for the families, and can I get on your calendar for fifteen minutes to walk your team through who qualifies?

When they push back

"Is this one of those things where you sign up and then get billed?"
No. It is a Medicare benefit, and the money goes from Medicare to us, not from you to us. What I will ask you to sign is a service agreement, and that is because if Medicare ever denies a visit we already made, we have to be able to bill for it. That is standard, and I will walk you through it line by line before you sign anything.
"She already has a doctor. I do not want to change anything."
Nothing changes. This does not replace her doctor or any specialist. It adds a dementia nurse who calls you once a month and a line you can call at night. Her doctor stays exactly where they are.
"I do not need help. I can take care of my own mother."
I believe you, and that is not what this is for. This is for the afternoon you have your own doctor's appointment, or the day your daughter gets married, or the week you get the flu. It is there so you can keep doing this for another two years instead of another six months.
"How much does the respite actually pay for?"
About $2,625 a year. In our hours that is roughly eighteen four-hour visits, so think of it as one afternoon every other week for a year, paid for. Some families use it all in one stretch for a trip instead. It is yours to spend either way.
"She is in assisted living. Does that count?"
It did until June, and it does not now. Medicare changed the rules on June 1 and limited the program to people in a private home or in independent living. I am sorry, because it is a real loss and it was not anybody here who decided it. If she is already enrolled, leave it alone, her medical group handles that and there is nothing you need to do. And call me anyway, because there are other things we can do for her.
"She is in a retirement community. Which one is that?"
The line is what her lease pays for, not what the place calls itself. If she has her own apartment and the community does not provide personal care, medication management, or supervision as part of what she pays them, that is independent living and she can qualify. If any of that is bundled into her rent, Medicare counts it as a residential care community and she cannot. Can you send me her residency agreement, or give me the community name and I will call them?
"He is on hospice."
Then he is not eligible for GUIDE, and I would not put you through the paperwork. If it turns out to be palliative care rather than hospice, that is different and he may qualify, so it is worth double checking which one it is.
"How long until it starts?"
Assessments are submitted on Fridays and people get aligned the Tuesday about a week and a half later. So realistically two to three weeks, sometimes a bit more. That is exactly why I do not want you waiting on it. Let us get care started now and let the paperwork catch up.

Never say

  • "Free care." It is about $2,625 a year of respite, not unlimited care. Families who hear "free" and then get a bill for their regular hours never trust us again.
  • "You are approved." We screen. CMS decides. Say "you look eligible" until alignment comes back.
  • "It replaces your doctor." It never does, and saying so gets the doctor angry at us.
  • Anything at all to someone on Medicare Advantage. Do not promise to check into it. They are not eligible, today, and a soft no now beats a hard no in three weeks.

What GUIDE actually is

Who qualifies

  • Diagnosed dementia or memory impairment
  • Traditional Medicare, not Medicare Advantage
  • Living in a private residence or independent living, and not in a nursing home, assisted living, memory care, or any other residential care community
  • Not on the Medicare hospice benefit (palliative care is fine)
  • Not enrolled in PACE
  • Medicaid and VA benefits are both fine alongside it

What the family gets

  • Care navigation. A monthly call from a trained dementia navigator: education, resources, brief counseling, advocacy. Required, not optional, and losing contact for several months can get them removed by CMS.
  • A 24/7 support line with telehealth. 888 466 9008, any hour, for urgent concerns that cannot wait for the doctor and for behavior nobody knows how to handle at 3am.
  • Respite. $2,625 a year for PY2026, which at the average rate is eighteen four-hour in-home visits, or 25 adult day sessions, or 8 days of 24-hour facility respite. Not everyone enrolled gets it. Respite needs a FAST stage of 5 to 7, a designated unpaid caregiver, and the caregiver's ZBI score, all taken during the assessment. A family early in the disease can be aligned with no respite hours at all and become eligible later as things progress.

Unalignment, and the trap in it

People fall out of GUIDE. Hospice admission, a move to a nursing home, enrolling in PACE, switching to a Medicare Advantage plan, or the patient asking to leave. CMS notifications do not always reach us quickly, which means we can deliver hours for somebody who was unaligned two weeks ago and not know it.

That is exactly why every GUIDE family signs a standard service agreement saying that if Medicare does not cover a visit, the visit is billable to them at our market rate. Not to be aggressive about collecting. To make sure nobody is shocked.

Services dated before the unalignment date can still be billed: Respite tab, Suspended, then enter it under the patient's name. Services on or after that date are not GUIDE eligible.

Caregiver training requirement

Any direct care worker providing GUIDE respite has to be 18 or older, pass a background check, and either hold state certification or complete the CMS-approved national training. Missouri has no separate licensure for this, so our people take the national training and we keep the certificate. CMS will not pay for respite if we cannot prove the worker was qualified, so the certificate has to be in the file before the first visit, not after.

Doing the assessment

This visit is where the money is. A patient who aligns pays us $200 for the assessment and opens up to eighteen respite visits. A patient whose paperwork does not hold up pays us nothing, because assessments are only paid if the client aligns. So the difference between a good assessment and a sloppy one is the whole job.

The two scores that decide everything

Alignment and respite are separate. Somebody can be aligned to GUIDE and get no respite hours at all. Respite needs three things, and two of them are measured by you during this visit:

  • FAST score of 5 to 7. The Functional Assessment Screening Tool.
  • ZBI, the Zarit Burden Index, filled out by the unpaid caregiver.
  • An unpaid caregiver designated on the profile. No caregiver, no ZBI, no respite.

Before you go: what the family needs to have ready

Send this the moment the visit is booked. Half the failed assessments are a missing diagnosis.

  • Medicare cards, theirs and the caregiver's if the caregiver also has traditional Medicare.
  • A current medication list, prescription and over the counter, with dose, frequency, prescribing doctor, and what it is for.
  • The dementia diagnosis. A physician letter, MyChart or MyMercy printout, VA paperwork, physician orders, or a history and physical from a community. An ICD-10 dementia diagnosis is required. If they cannot find one, go anyway. Continuum's medical director can issue a general diagnosis from the assessment, but it is the slow way and not the preferred one.
  • Power of attorney, if there is one. Optional but useful.
  • A family caregiver who knows the day to day must be there. Not optional. You cannot score the FAST off the patient alone, and the ZBI is theirs to fill out.
Text or email it when you book the visit.

FAST, and the mistake everyone makes

FAST stages the dementia. A 5 is the floor for respite, and a 5 means the person needs help choosing their clothing. Below that, no respite.

  • Score cognition, not physical ability. This is the one people get wrong. Arthritis, a stroke, Parkinson's: none of it counts here. If she cannot button her shirt because her hands hurt, that is not a FAST point. If she cannot decide what to put on, that is.
  • Ask the caregiver, not the patient. A person with dementia will tell you they are managing fine. The family member who knows the day to day verifies every answer.
  • Make it match everything else. Crosby flags an assessment where the FAST does not line up with the ADL and IADL answers, or where the cognitive screening says moderate to severe but the FAST says they barely need help. That inconsistency sends it back.

ZBI, the caregiver's questionnaire

  • Hand it over and step back. It asks a person how much caring for their mother is costing them, and nobody answers that honestly with their mother sitting there. Paper, tablet, laptop, whatever you have, and let them do it alone.
  • Every question has to be answered or the assessment will not submit. Check it before you leave the house, not from the car.
  • If the ZBI does not appear at all, stop. That means no unpaid caregiver was designated when the profile was created, and without one the client cannot qualify for respite. Call Continuum or Crosby right then and have the caregiver added, rather than finishing the visit and discovering it later.

The cognitive screening

  • Follow the instructions as written. Answers are pre-populated for you.
  • Repeat a question once, never twice.
  • The list of ten words is the exception: it cannot be repeated at all. Read it slowly, a full second between each word. Rushing it produces a low score that will not match the rest of the assessment, and then the whole thing gets questioned.

Notes, and why they matter to somebody else

There are note boxes at the bottom of most sections and a Client and Caregiver Goals box at the end of the in-home assessment. A Care Navigator builds the care plan from what you write there. What sounds like a throwaway detail to you, the daughter works nights, he will not let anyone but her help him shower, is exactly what makes a care plan usable instead of generic.

Reading the eligibility answer

Once an assessment is in, respite eligibility shows right in the assessment summary. Two things you will see:

  • Pending. Submitted and waiting on CMS. Normal, and not a yes. Keep saying "looks eligible" to the family until it stops being pending.
  • Not Eligible, with a reason attached, usually FAST stage criteria or caregiver status. FAST is the dementia staging scale, so somebody can be too early in the disease to qualify. That one is not on our nine questions because a clinician decides it, not us.

Performance years, and the trap in them

Respite budgets run on a performance year that resets every July 1, not the calendar year. PY2026 began July 1, 2026 with $2,625 per beneficiary. Unused money does not carry over.

So when you bill for a visit that happened before July 1, switch the year selector in the Respite module to Performance Year 2025 first. Enter it under 2026 and it comes out of the wrong budget. The selector sits at the top of the Respite page, next to the patient filter.

Two things follow from that. A family saving their hours for a rainy day loses them on June 30, so spread the visits through the year. And somebody who starts in May has two budgets available inside three months, which is worth knowing before you tell them what they have.

Rapid Pay is a loan, not a payment

CMS itself takes 45 to 120+ days. Crosby fronts us the money in 3 to 5 weeks instead, and every partner is enrolled. That is genuinely useful for cash flow, and it comes with a string worth reading twice:

Submitting an invoice enrolls us in the expedited payment program, and any amount CMS does not reimburse has to be paid back by us.

So a visit billed for somebody who was quietly unaligned, or hours billed past the budget, is not a denied claim that simply never pays. It is money we received and then owe. That is the reason to check Budget Spent before scheduling and to bill weekly rather than in a monthly pile.
