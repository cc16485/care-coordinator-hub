HomeTogether TV, included at 20+ hours

Everything you need to sell it, in the words to use. Every box has a copy button.

The offer, in one sentence

The sentence
Private-pay families receiving 20 or more hours of care a week get HomeTogether TV included at no charge, a $99 a month value, so they can see their mom on her own television instead of just hearing how she's doing.

Why we do this instead of discounting. Two dollars an hour off a 20-hour client costs $172 every month, forever, and nobody remembers it. This costs about $50, it is something they can see and touch, and it is the one thing the agency down the road cannot match. Never discount the rate when you can give this instead.

What to say in a consultation

Do not lead with it. Earn the conversation first, then use it to close.

At the table, once you know they need 15 to 20 hours
One more thing, and this is the part families usually don't expect. At 20 hours a week we include HomeTogether TV at no charge. It plugs into her television, and you can call her from your phone and she doesn't have to do anything, it just comes up on the screen. Up to ten of you at once, so the grandkids can be on it Sunday. Families tell me the hardest part isn't the care, it's being three hours away and not knowing how she really sounded that day. This is the thing that fixes that. It's $99 a month on its own. At 20 hours it's just part of what you get.
When they are at 15 or 16 hours and hesitating
Can I show you something? At 16 hours you're at [$X] a month. At 20 you're at [$Y], and the TV comes with it. The difference is about an hour a day, and honestly, an hour a day is usually the difference between her managing and her doing well. I'm not trying to sell you hours you don't need. But if you were already wondering whether 16 was enough, that's the version I'd pick.
When they are comparing us to another agency
Take your time and talk to a couple of places, honestly. When you do, ask each one what's included at 20 hours a week. That's the question that usually makes the difference clear.

Targeting that works for home care: adults 45 to 70, within 25 miles of Springfield, and let the algorithm do the rest. Do not layer on lots of interests, it starves the audience. Send them to the offer page, not the homepage. Optimise for the consultation booking, not the click. Start at $15 a day per ad for a week before you judge anything.
Two rules Facebook actually enforces. You cannot target people by health condition, and you cannot write copy that implies you know something personal about the reader. "Does your mother have dementia?" gets rejected. "Families caring for a parent with dementia" is fine. Keep it about us and about the offer, never about a diagnosis you are guessing they have.

Ad artwork: what to have made

Paste any of these straight into a design tool. Every one assumes our palette: navy #0D365F, honey #F0A63A, cream #faf9f6, serif headlines, no stock-photo gloss.

The single rule. The picture has to show a face on a television and an older person looking at it. That is the whole product in one frame. Everything else, the device, the logo, the price, is decoration. If a designer sends back a smiling stock senior holding a tablet, send it back.

Sizes to ask for, every time

  • 1080 × 1350 (4:5) — the main feed size, takes up the most screen on a phone. Make this one first.
  • 1080 × 1080 (1:1) — square, for the feed and for Instagram.
  • 1080 × 1920 (9:16) — Stories and Reels. Keep everything important in the middle, the top and bottom get covered.
Brief 1 — the guilt of distance
Photograph, shot from behind and slightly to the side of an older woman sitting in her own living room chair. We see the back of her head and shoulder, and past her, the television. On the screen is a woman in her forties mid-sentence, clearly on a video call, in an ordinary kitchen somewhere else. Warm lamp light, late afternoon. A real room: a crocheted blanket, a side table with a coaster and reading glasses, family photos on the wall. No text on the image at all, or at most one line in the lower third: "See her face." Serif, cream, on a soft navy gradient so it stays readable. Feels like: a documentary still. Slightly imperfect, real furniture, real clutter. Avoid: white backgrounds, staged smiles at the camera, anything that looks like a hospital or a brochure, and any device that is not a television.
Brief 2 — the switch
Typographic, almost no photo. Deep navy background (#0D365F). One question centred in large cream serif, big enough to read at a glance while scrolling: "What's included at 20 hours a week?" Underneath, small, in honey (#F0A63A): "Ask your agency. Then ask us." Bottom edge: the Caring Companions logo, small, and nothing else. In the lower right corner, a small photograph, no bigger than a quarter of the frame, of a television showing a family on a video call, with soft rounded corners so it reads as an inset rather than a background. Feels like: a plain question asked confidently, not an advertisement. Avoid: exclamation marks, starbursts, "LIMITED TIME", and any comparison table.
Brief 3 — the grandchildren
The television is the whole frame, shot straight on, filling most of the image. On the screen is a video call grid with four or five faces of different ages, including at least two children, all mid-laugh, clearly in different homes. In the bottom of the frame, out of focus, the back of an older person's head and the top of an armchair, so you understand who is watching. Text across the top in cream serif, kept short: "Ten grandchildren. One television. No passwords." Small honey band along the bottom: "Included free at 20+ hours a week of care." Feels like: a Sunday afternoon, noisy and happy. Avoid: a perfect symmetrical grid of professional headshots. Make the faces look like a real family on a real call, one too close to the camera, one in a doorway.

If you want one video instead

Brief 4 — 15 seconds, vertical, no words spoken
1080 × 1920, fifteen seconds, silent, captions burned in because most people watch with the sound off. 0 to 3 sec: a dark television in a quiet living room. An older woman is doing something ordinary nearby, folding a towel, turning a page. 3 to 6 sec: the screen lights up. A face appears. She looks up. She has not touched anything. 6 to 11 sec: she is talking to the screen and smiling. Cut to two more faces joining the call, then a child waving. 11 to 15 sec: cut to cream. Navy serif: "Included free at 20+ hours a week." Then the logo and mo-care.com/hometogether-included. Captions, in order: "She doesn't answer it." / "She doesn't unlock anything." / "It just comes on." / "Included at 20 hours a week." No music with lyrics. Nothing dramatic. Let it be quiet.
Where the real footage comes from. Once a family has a device and has said yes on their video page, that is your ad. A real Springfield living room beats anything a designer can build, and you already have the consent process for it. Until then, use the briefs above and swap in the real thing the moment you have it.
Never use a client's face in an ad without their written yes on file, and never use a photograph of a real client's home in a way that could identify where they live. The release on the Share Your Story page covers video. A photo taken on a coordinator's phone at a visit does not.

The rules, so nobody promises the wrong thing

  • Private pay only. Long-term care insurance and privately paid VA benefits count. Medicaid-funded care does not, and this is not negotiable, so never imply otherwise to a family or a partner.
  • 20 hours a week or more, on the schedule, not on a hope.
  • Ships after 30 days of care, not on the day they sign.
  • It stays our equipment. It comes back when care ends. $300 only if it is never returned.
  • Below 20 hours for two months running, we ask whether they want to keep it at $99 or send it back. Nobody gets a surprise bill.
  • Internet in the home is required, and it needs an HDMI port. Almost every TV from the last fifteen years has one.
Never say "free device" to a Medicaid family, even as a maybe. If a family is on Medicaid and asks, the answer is: that offer is for our private-pay families, but let me tell you what we can do. Then talk about the care.
